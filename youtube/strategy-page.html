Identity

Two of these are deliberately narrow, two deliberately abstract. Narrow names sell the first video harder. Abstract names survive year three, when you want to make an episode about a cave painting and a name about missing people no longer fits.

The strongest three

Recommended — Last Known Position

It states the format, not the topic. Every episode is the reconstruction of a record up to the point the record stops — which covers disappearances, abandoned places, unexplained signals and objects with no origin, without locking you into missing persons.

It carries dread without a single adjective. No "dark," no "mysterious." Restraint in the name is a promise about the narration. It gives you a sign-off and a franchise: episodes close on a card reading LAST KNOWN POSITION over coordinates, and series names fall out of it — Sea Files, Cold Rooms, Signal Logs. It stacks as three short lines in a thumbnail corner, and LKP works as a small mark on every frame.

Risk: three words is long for a handle. Secure the handle everywhere on day one.

Runner-up — Grid North

Grid north is the direction the vertical lines on a map point. It is not true north and not magnetic north — every map you have used is quietly a few degrees off, and navigators correct for it. As a name it says: the accepted version is slightly wrong, and this channel measures the difference.

The better ten-year name and the weaker cold-start name. It tells a scrolling stranger nothing, so packaging carries 100% of the work. Take it only if you are confident there.

Third — The Quiet Record

The strongest positioning name of the ten. The competitive gap in this niche is that AI-assisted mystery channels are loud, padded and unsourced. A name opening with "quiet" is a thesis statement. Its weakness is selling that quietness to a hype-trained feed.

Target audience

A 25–44 year old in the US, UK, Canada or Australia who watches long-form video the way their parents watched documentaries — on a TV or a propped-up phone, in the evening, alone, usually between 8pm and 1am. Roughly 65/35 male, with the female share rising on human-story episodes and falling on hardware episodes.

They already watch LEMMiNO, Fascinating Horror, Barely Sociable, Scary Interesting, Plainly Difficult, Wendigoon. They also watch actual documentaries, and they will compare you to those, not to other faceless channels.

What they want

To learn something true. The dopamine is "I now know a real thing most people don't." They forward it to one friend with the words "how have we never heard of this."

To be treated as an adult. No repeated explanations, no throat-clearing.

To be told what isn't known. The most under-served desire in the niche.

What makes them leave

Padding. They can smell a story stretched to hit ten minutes.

Performed emotion the material hasn't earned.

AI tells: melting hands, six-fingered crowds, breaths in the wrong places.

Being lied to. One fabricated detail loses that viewer permanently — and they say so in the comments, where everyone can see.

The anti-audience. You are not making content for the "scary top 10" teen audience, the paranormal-believer audience who want confirmation rather than evidence, or the conspiracy audience. Serving any of them changes your comment section within a month and you do not get it back.

The one accent

The rule that matters

The accent appears once per frame, on the thing you want looked at. A pin on a map. A circle round the anomalous detail. One word in a document. If amber is on two things at once, it points at nothing.

Blacks are lifted slightly, never crushed to pure black — that reads as film rather than as a PNG, and it stops OLED phone screens swallowing your shadow detail. This page is set in the palette, so you can see it working.

Typography — three faces, no fourth

RoleFaceWhy
Display / titlesArchivo Bold, tightNeo-grotesque, authoritative, condenses well into thumbnails
Data / cardsJetBrains MonoDates, coordinates, case numbers. Monospace is the record aesthetic
Quotes / documentsNewsreader ItalicReads as printed page, not as UI
SubtitlesInter MediumLegibility first

The monospace does more identity work than the display face. Resist adding a fourth "atmospheric" font.

The grade — and the anti-slop tool

Desaturate to 70–80%, then let amber sit at full strength: the eye reads it as colour has been removed from the world except where it matters. Lift the black point, roll off highlights with a soft halation bloom, hold a constant 35mm grain.

Single most effective technique

Run one grain-and-grade layer over everything — generated shots, archival film and graphics alike. A shared grain makes three incompatible source types feel like one piece of film. It does more for believability than any prompt.

Motion, devices, sound

Push-ins of 2–4% over 8–10 seconds, nothing faster. Average shot length 4–7 seconds — roughly double the faceless norm; slow cutting reads as confidence. No whip pans, no zoom punches, no glitch transitions. Cuts land on the end of a thought.

Five recurring devices make the channel recognisable in three seconds: the record card (mono: place, date, time, coordinates) opening each act; the map system on one fixed template; the document plate with one line circled in amber; the timeline strip; and the closing frame — mark, coordinates, one unanswered question, held four seconds in silence.

Sound is a three-layer bed — a low drone, a texture layer, and a pulse that only enters during escalation — with one sub-bass hit per act card. Narration at −16 LUFS, bed ducked 10–12 dB beneath. Master to −14 LUFS integrated. If you recognise a music track from another channel in this niche, do not use it.

Tone of narration

The persona

Not a storyteller. Not a host. A researcher who found something in the record and cannot stop thinking about it. Calm, precise, occasionally dry, genuinely uncertain about the parts that are uncertain — interested in the story rather than in your reaction to it. They never sound like they are trying to keep you watching, which is why you keep watching.

Short sentences, averaging 10–15 words. 150–160 words per minute — slower than the platform norm of 175–190; slow reads as authority and gives visuals room. Concrete over abstract always: not "conditions were harsh" but "−11°C, and the nearest road was fourteen miles away." One adjective per idea. Past tense for the record, present tense for reconstruction — switching into present for a 30-second run is your strongest tension device, so use it three times a video, never more.

The epistemic ladder — the signature

RegisterPhrasingExample
RECORD"The record shows…" / "According to the inquiry…""The inquiry recorded that the radio was tuned to the international distress frequency."
REPORTED"It was reported at the time…" / "…appears in the press but not in the file.""Later accounts describe a briefcase. The inventory does not mention one."
THEORY"The most common explanation is… It explains X, but not Y.""A rogue wave accounts for the list. It does not account for the missing cargo."

Say "nobody knows" out loud at least once in every video. Carry the register as a small mono tag in the corner of the frame. It costs nothing, viewers screenshot it, and it becomes the brand.

Never write

Welcome back to the channel · In this video we'll be exploring · But first · Little did they know · What happened next will · You won't believe · Buckle up · Let that sink in · Truth is stranger than fiction · Chilling · Bone-chilling · Spine-tingling · Shrouded in mystery · Little is known about · To this day · Some say · Many believe · One thing is certain · The plot thickens · Delve · Testament to · In the annals of

Length and cadence

Runtime — 11 to 14 minutes

Over 8 minutes unlocks multiple mid-rolls. 11–14 is long enough for a real three-act reconstruction and short enough that a new channel can hold 45–55% average viewing — a 25-minute film from a channel with no trust bleeds through the middle third, and low AVD is a worse signal than a shorter film watched well.

Never pad to hit a runtime. A tight nine beats a bloated thirteen, and this audience can tell instantly.

Every 10–13 videos, make one 25–35 minute flagship. Those get compared to real documentaries and are what people link when recommending the channel.

Schedule — weekly, Friday 16:00 UK

Friday afternoon publishes into US East lunchtime, UK evening, and the whole weekend for the recommendation system to work with. Mystery viewing peaks Friday to Sunday night. The slot must be identical every week — returning viewers matter more here than in most niches and need a habit to form around.

Do not publish until four finished videos exist. Non-negotiable. The buffer is what protects the schedule the first time you get ill or a story collapses under research.

No Shorts until eight long-forms are live, then two a week maximum, as standalone strange facts — never clips.

§02One structure, every video

The Formula

A fixed structure isn't creative laziness. It removes the blank page, makes research targeted, and turns retention graphs into diagnostics — "we lose people in Act III" is actionable; "the video underperformed" is not.

Timings assume 12 minutes at ~155 wpm.

010:00–0:25Cold openOne image, one impossible detail, one question you refuse to answer60–70w
020:25–0:33Title cardBrand beat. Sub-bass hit and the record card0–12w
030:33–2:30SetupPlace, time, people, and the normal state of the world~295w
042:30–5:00The ruptureThe thing that breaks, delivered in discovery order~385w
055:00–8:15Escalation ×3Each beat closes one explanation and opens two questions~490w
068:15–10:30RevelationThe strongest honest answer — and precisely what it fails to cover~355w

0811:50–12:00End cardCoordinates, mark, next video. Four seconds of silence—

The 25 seconds that decide the video

Target: hold 75% or more of viewers to 0:30. Start inside a moment — a specific person, at a specific place, at a specific time, doing a specific thing. Not context. Not a summary. Not the theme.

The shape: the anchor (a time, a place, a person, delivered flat) → the ordinary (what was normal about this moment — that's what makes the break land) → the rupture, stated plainly (the flatter you say it, the louder it is) → the withheld question (state what's missing; don't promise to answer it).

Worked example — the Ghost Blimp

At 6:03 on the morning of the sixteenth of August, 1942, a US Navy airship lifted off from Treasure Island in San Francisco Bay. Two men were aboard. It was an anti-submarine patrol — a slow circuit over the water, the kind they had flown dozens of times.

Four hours later the airship came down in a residential street in Daly City. A fire crew climbed into the gondola.

The radio worked. The life raft was still stowed. The parachutes had not been touched.

Both men were gone.

No adjective does the work. The strange fact arrives as inventory. A four-word sentence lands in silence.

The two rules that separate this from a listicle

Rupture — discovery order, not understanding order

The people who found the scene did not know what they were looking at. Put the viewer in their position and the strangeness arrives naturally, without a narrator having to announce that it is strange. Lead with physical facts. Inventory is your best friend — a list of ordinary objects in an impossible arrangement beats any adjective.

Revelation — steelman the boring answer

Give the strongest available explanation honestly, including when it's mundane. Channels that refuse to engage with the boring answer get destroyed in the comments and never build authority. State it as its best advocate would, show what it explains well, then name the one specific thing it doesn't. Residual mystery is far more powerful for having survived scrutiny.

Before and after

AI draft

The discovery of the abandoned vessel sent shockwaves through the maritime community. What investigators found aboard the Joyita was nothing short of baffling — a ship that should have been impossible to abandon, yet its entire complement of twenty-five souls had seemingly vanished into thin air. It's important to note that the vessel was equipped with a cork-lined hull, making it virtually unsinkable. And yet, chillingly, not a single person was ever found.

Rewritten

The Joyita was found on the tenth of November, drifting six hundred miles from where she should have been. She was listing heavily to port. The deck was awash.

Twenty-five people had been aboard. None of them were.

This mattered because of how the ship was built. Her hull was lined with cork — a conversion from her days as a research vessel. Waterlogged, she would sit low and uncomfortable. She would not go down. The crew had every reason to stay with her.

Nobody has ever been found.

92 words became 96 — this is not about brevity. It's about replacing commentary with facts, replacing "baffling" with the actual reason it is baffling, and letting a four-word sentence do the emotional work.

Source tiers

TierWhat it isRule
T1Inquiry reports, court records, ship logs, declassified files, contemporary newspaper scans, peer-reviewed papersAlways preferred. Cite on screen
T2Books with citations, national-broadcaster investigations, museum and archive publicationsUsable. Trace back to T1 where possible
T3Unsourced mainstream articles, encyclopaedia entries, other YouTube videosNever a sole source. Leads only
T4Forums, wikis, blogs, social postsLeads only. Never cited, never repeated
The thing that gets you caught

A detail that exists only downstream of one 2011 blog post. This niche is riddled with them. When every source repeats the same sentence with the same phrasing, they are all copying one another. Find the original or drop the detail. The same applies to numbers — casualty figures, distances and durations inflate as stories are retold. Go to the inquiry.

Handling death, crime and tragedy

Editorial

Name victims — use their names, not "the victim." Never describe injuries in forensic detail. No crime-scene photographs of identifiable remains, ever — use the exterior, the road, the empty room. Don't speculate about a real person's last moments beyond what the record supports. Where families are living, assume they may watch.

Staying fully monetised

State the manner of death once, clinically, and don't return to it. Keep the most sensitive terms out of the first thirty seconds, the title and the thumbnail. Frame the video as an investigation of a record — which is what it is. Self-certify honestly; a carefully written video generally comes through fine, and one that hides its subject and gets caught does worse.

Originality

Non-negotiable

Never work from another YouTube video. Not as a source, not as a structure, not as a starting point. Watch competitors only after the script is locked, and only to check you haven't accidentally converged. Build every episode from the documents up — if your dossier contains only secondary retellings, you don't yet have an episode. Your structure, your sequencing and your prose are the product. The facts are everybody's.

§04Sourcing · Generation · Maps · Sound

Visual System

The goal is a film that happens to have been assembled from generated, archival and graphic material — not a slideshow of AI images. Two principles do most of the work: mixed sourcing, and one grade over everything.

Shot mix — target proportions per 12-minute video

TypeShareWhat it is
AI cinematic25%Atmospheric establishing shots, interiors, weather, period texture
Archival / public domain25%Real film, real photographs, newsreel, official imagery
Documents & photographs15%Reports, letters, logs, newspaper pages, ID photos
Maps & diagrams15%Location, route, timeline, cutaway, scale comparison
Typographic cards10%Record cards, act cards, quotations, timestamps
Texture & abstract10%Water, smoke, grain, static, macro detail
Three rules on top of the mix

Two AI shots maximum in a row. Break with a document, a map, a card or a texture.

The three most important moments must not be AI shots. The rupture, the key evidence and the revelation should each be carried by a real document, photograph, map or card. Real material at the emotional peaks makes the whole film feel real.

Every claim on screen has a visual that supports it. If narration says "tuned to 2182 kilohertz," show the frequency.

Rights discipline: "old" does not mean public domain, and rules differ by country. Log the licence and source URL for every asset. If you can't establish rights, generate an equivalent and label it.

Ethical labelling — non-negotiable

If you recreate a document, a photograph or a scene with AI, put a small mono RECREATION tag in the corner of that shot. It costs nothing, and it is the difference between a documentary and a forgery.

The AI method — stills first, motion second

Don't generate video from text. Generate a still you fully control, then animate it. You can iterate on a still for pennies, you avoid temporal artefacts, and you keep a consistent look across a hundred shots.

Animate by 2.5D parallax (cut the still into 3–4 depth layers moving at different rates — the workhorse, and it looks better than most generated video), slow drift, or 3–5 second image-to-video for shots that genuinely need it. Then composite real grain, dust, light leaks and rain plates over the top. That single trick does more for believability than a better prompt.

The look-bible suffix — appended to every prompt

Shot on 35mm film, anamorphic, shallow depth of field, natural available light only, heavily desaturated cold palette, single warm amber practical light source, lifted shadows, soft halation on highlights, fine film grain, slight lens vignette, documentary photography, no text, no watermark, no people looking at camera.
The AI-human problem

No AI-generated close-ups of faces. Ever. People appear as backs of heads, silhouettes, figures at distance, hands only, obscured by weather or glass, or out of focus in the foreground. Crowds only in silhouette — count the fingers before you use the shot.

When you need a real person's face, use their real photograph. If none exists, use an empty chair, their signature, their file card, or their name on a card. Absence is more powerful than a fabricated face anyway.

Maps — the most under-priced asset you have

Cheap, unmistakably real, information-carrying, and no AI artefact can appear in them. Build one template and reuse it forever: dark basemap, bone contours, mono labels, one amber marker, a route that draws on over 2–3 seconds. A scale bar and a north arrow on every map, always — it reads as rigour.

Highest-value tool on the entire list

Google Earth Studio. Free, browser-based, keyframes real satellite terrain into cinematic flyovers and exports straight to After Effects. For any story with a location, learn it in an afternoon and you'll use it in every episode after.

§05Idea → Upload in 6–8 hours

Workflow

The first three videos will take 14–18 hours each. That is expected and not wasted — you are building the template library, and the library buys the speed back. By video six you should be at 8 hours; by video ten, 6.

Principle one

AI drafts, human decides. Every AI output is an input to a human judgement, never a finished artefact. The two places this is inviolable: the fact-check, and the final script pass.

Principle two

Templates over effort. Anything you do twice becomes a template. Speed comes from never solving a solved problem again.

00
Idea bank

Weekly 30-minute sweep of archives, newspaper databases and recent scientific papers. Score every idea /25 on curiosity gap, source depth, visual availability, competition and ad safety. Only 18+ gets commissioned — a 16 you love will underperform and you'll blame the algorithm.

30m / wk
01
Research

AI sweep for chronology, cast, investigations and — crucially — the claims that can't be traced to a primary source. Then hunt the actual documents. Then load only those documents into a grounded retrieval tool and interrogate those. That last step is what eliminates hallucinated detail.

90 min
02
Script

Beat sheet first — if you can't fill the Revelation line, you don't have an episode. AI drafts beat by beat against the word budgets. Then 45 minutes of human line-level rewriting, which is where everything the audience responds to comes from. Read aloud with a timer.

90 min
03
Fact check

Fresh session, different model. Extract every claim as a list. Rate confidence adversarially. Then a human checks every claim rated 3 or below against the primary documents. Each claim gets a source, an epistemic marker, or the cut.

45 min
04
Shot list

Split the locked script into 90–110 shots, tag each by type, check the mix percentages, and write the archive query or the full prompt now. This turns the edit into assembly rather than invention — where most of the saving lives.

30 min
05
Assets

Batch by type, not chronology. All stills in one run, all archive downloads in one run, all maps in one session. Name files by shot number so the edit self-assembles.

60–90 min
06
Voiceover

Pronunciation list first. Render paragraph by paragraph so a bad line can be re-rolled without flattening the whole file. Then edit the audio — extend act-break pauses, remove wrong-place breaths. Twenty minutes here is the difference between "AI narration" and "narration."

30 min
07
Edit

Eight passes in fixed order: lay VO → assembly → timing → graphics → sound → grade → captions → one uninterrupted watch. Working out of order is what makes edits take a day.

120–180 min
08
Packaging

Three genuinely different thumbnails — image-led, artefact-led, text-led — judged at 210×118 pixels. Five titles down to one. A description whose first two lines carry the search terms, real chapter names, and a visible source list.

45 min
09
Publish + 48h loop

Reply to every comment for four hours. Pin a real extra fact with its source. At 48 hours read the retention graph and commit to one change. At 7 and 30 days, swap the thumbnail if CTR is under 4%.

§06Twenty commissioned stories, packaged

The Slate

All real, documented events with traceable primary sources. Every one still needs the full research and fact-check process before scripting — these are commissioning briefs, not verified copy.

Every title must pass six tests

1. Curiosity, not concealment — state a real strange fact and stop. 2. It must still feel true after watching. 3. Under 60 characters where possible. 4. A concrete noun in the first three words. 5. No question mark unless the video answers it. 6. Say it out loud — if it sounds like a YouTube title, rewrite it; if it sounds like something a person would say to a friend, keep it.

The Ghost Blimp — L-8, 1942

Premise

A US Navy airship on a routine wartime patrol drifted back over San Francisco and landed in a suburban street with everything aboard intact and both crewmen gone, and the Navy's inquiry never established how they left.

Why it opens the channel

One unforgettable image with real photographs behind it. A scene that is strange purely as an inventory — nothing gruesome, nothing to soften. A formal board of inquiry means genuine primary documents. Abundant WWII archival film. Completely ad-safe. And almost nobody has told it well.

Five titles

  2. In 1942, a US Navy Blimp Came Home Empty ◂ recommended
  3. The Ghost Blimp of Daly City
  4. Two Men Vanished From a Sealed Airship. It Flew Home Without Them.

Why that one

Fifty characters. A date to anchor it as real history, a concrete object in the first four words, and "came home empty" implies everything without a dramatic adjective. Title 4 tests well but "vanished" is the most exhausted word in the niche.

Thumbnail

★ 02Strange discoverySearch demand

Roopkund — The Lake of Bones

Premise

Hundreds of human skeletons lie in a glacial lake five kilometres up in the Indian Himalaya, and when geneticists finally sequenced them in 2019 the answer was far stranger than a single disaster.

Why it's high

One of the best reveals in the genre — for seventy years everyone assumed one catastrophe, and the DNA showed something nobody had considered. The site photographs are extraordinary and instantly thumbnail-able. Anchored in peer-reviewed science, which makes it unimpeachable. And it ends with a question the science made bigger.

The First 1,000

The maths that makes this tractable

Monetisation needs 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 valid public watch hours in twelve months. There's also a lower tier at 500 subscribers and 3,000 watch hours that unlocks memberships and Super Thanks — hit that first and treat it as the real halfway marker, because it's the first point the channel can earn anything.

StepFigure

The consequence — and it simplifies everything

In this niche, watch hours and subscribers arrive at roughly the same moment. You don't need two strategies. Optimise for one number — total minutes watched — and both thresholds fall together. Anything that raises views but not watch time actively makes the target harder.

The single target: 50,000 long-form views.

The realistic shape of it

Most channels that get there do it the same way: eight to fifteen videos of modest performance while the recommendation system works out who the audience is, and then one video that breaks out and drags everything behind it.

You can't schedule the breakout. You can only make sure there are enough well-packaged episodes for the algorithm to test, and that when one does break, the next-best video is right there in the end screen. A plausible distribution across your first twenty: eleven or twelve at 300–1,500 views, five or six at 2,000–6,000, one or two at 15,000–60,000.

Expect four to nine months. Anyone promising faster is selling something.

The four things that actually move the number

01
Repackage relentlessly

Highest return, lowest effort. Check CTR at 48 hours, 7 days and 30 days; below ~4% from browse means packaging is the bottleneck, not the film. Swap in one of the two thumbnails you already made. A video from month one, retitled in month six with everything you've learned, is the cheapest new video you will ever make.

15m / wk
02
Fix retention, one variable at a time

CTR gets people in; retention decides whether anyone else is shown the video. Target 75%+ at 0:30 and 45–55% AVD. Change exactly one thing per video and write it down. Ten videos of tracked single-variable changes teaches you more about your specific audience than any amount of general advice.

20m / video
03
Build sessions, not views

A viewer who watches two videos back-to-back is worth far more than two viewers watching one each. Choose the end-screen video deliberately — the one whose thumbnail pairs best with the mood the viewer is in at the end of this film, not the newest one. Named series playlists on autoplay. Not about the algorithm. A hundred-view video with fifteen real replies from you builds a group of people who come back for video two — and video two is where channels either start or die. Those first two hundred people are the entire asset.

Post the artefact, not the link

Most communities where this content lands ban self-promotion, and they're right to. For every video, produce one asset that stands alone — the map you built, a comparison of two photographs, a scan of the key document with the relevant line highlighted. Post that natively, with the story told properly in the post itself and your sources in a comment.

People who want more will find the channel. People who wanted a video link were never going to watch twelve minutes anyway.

What not to do: buy anything, join engagement pods, sub-for-sub, or post links where they aren't wanted. Beyond breaking platform policy, inflated views from uninterested people wreck your retention and CTR signals — and those signals are the only thing deciding whether YouTube shows the video to the people who would have loved it.

The contract with yourself

Thirteen videos before you are allowed to conclude anything. The most common way channels like this fail is changing direction at video five, then again at video nine, so the recommendation system never learns who to show them to.

Beyond 1,000

Ad revenue is the floor, not the plan. The first paid upgrade is a human narrator — the biggest single quality jump available, and it removes the last obvious AI tell. The second is a researcher: research is the most delegable stage and the one most responsible for episode quality, so buy hours there before buying editing hours.

Sponsorship arrives around 10–20k subscribers and pays substantially better than ads; place reads after the Revelation, never before, and never take a sponsor whose product contradicts the channel's epistemic promise. Memberships work for this audience specifically if what they buy is the research — the source documents, the claims sheet, the cut material. That's a real product for exactly this kind of person, and it costs you nothing because you already made all of it.

And the flagship documentaries are the long game. A 35-minute film every quarter is what turns a YouTube channel into something people describe as a studio.
