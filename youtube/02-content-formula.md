# 02 — The Content Formula

One structure, every video. The point of a fixed structure is not creative laziness — it is
that it removes the blank page, makes research targeted, and lets you diagnose retention
problems precisely ("we lose people in Act III" is actionable; "the video underperformed"
is not).

---

## The beat sheet

Timings assume a 12-minute video at ~155 words per minute (≈1,860 words). Scale
proportionally for other lengths.

| # | Beat | Time | Words | Job |
|---|------|------|-------|-----|
| 1 | **Cold open / Hook** | 0:00–0:25 | 60–70 | One image, one impossible detail, one question you refuse to answer |
| 2 | **Title card** | 0:25–0:33 | 0–12 | Brand beat. Sound design. Optional single line over it |
| 3 | **Setup** | 0:33–2:30 | 280–310 | Place, time, people, and the normal state of the world |
| 4 | **The rupture** | 2:30–5:00 | 370–400 | The thing that breaks, delivered in discovery order |
| 5 | **Escalation** | 5:00–8:15 | 480–520 | Three beats, each closing one door and opening two |
| 6 | **Revelation** | 8:15–10:30 | 340–370 | The strongest honest explanation — and what it fails to cover |
| 7 | **Ending** | 10:30–11:50 | 200–230 | The unanswered question. Return to the hook image. Stop |
| 8 | **End card** | 11:50–12:00 | 0 | Coordinates, mark, next video. Four seconds of silence |

---

## Beat 1 — Cold open (0:00–0:25)

The 25 seconds that decide the video. Target: **hold 75% or more of viewers to 0:30.**

**Start inside a moment.** A specific person, at a specific place, at a specific time,
doing a specific thing. Not context. Not a summary. Not the theme.

The formula that works:

1. **Sentence 1–2: the anchor.** A time, a place, a person. Flat delivery.
2. **Sentence 3–5: the ordinary.** What was normal about this moment. Keeps it human and
   makes the break land.
3. **Sentence 6–7: the rupture, stated plainly.** The single strange fact. Never dressed up
   — the flatter you say it, the louder it is.
4. **Sentence 8: the withheld question.** State what is missing. Do not promise to answer it.

**Worked example** (Ghost Blimp, L-8):

> At 6:03 in the morning on the sixteenth of August, 1942, a US Navy airship lifted off
> from Treasure Island in San Francisco Bay. Two men were aboard. It was a routine
> anti-submarine patrol — a slow, boring circuit over the water, the kind they'd flown
> dozens of times.
>
> Four hours later the airship came down in a residential street in Daly City, California.
> It settled onto the road between two houses. A fire crew climbed into the gondola.
>
> Everything was still there. The radio worked. The life raft was still stowed. The
> parachutes hadn't been touched. The engines had been switched off, deliberately, from
> inside.
>
> The two men were gone. [hold]

That is 116 words — slightly long, and worth trimming to 90 — but it demonstrates the
shape: no adjectives doing the work, the strange fact delivered as inventory, and a final
four-word sentence that lands in silence.

**Never open with:** the channel name, "in this video," a definition, a rhetorical question,
a montage of the whole story, a statistic, or the word "imagine."

---

## Beat 2 — Title card (0:25–0:33)

Eight seconds, one sub-bass hit, the wordmark, the episode title in Archivo, and the record
card: place, date, coordinates. Then straight into Setup.

This exists for one reason: it converts the cold open from "a video that started weirdly"
into "an episode of something." Channels without a consistent title beat feel like a
collection of uploads.

Optional single narration line over it, if the story needs one orienting fact the hook
couldn't carry.

---

## Beat 3 — Setup (0:33–2:30)

The most-skipped beat and the reason most videos in this niche feel hollow. You are not
delivering background. You are making the viewer care whether these people are found.

- **One human detail per named person.** What they were like, what they'd done that morning,
  what they'd said the day before. One line each. This is the whole difference between "two
  crew members" and two people.
- **Establish the normal.** The stakes of a disappearance are set entirely by how ordinary
  the day was.
- **Plant the withheld detail.** Mention one fact in passing that will matter enormously in
  Act III, and do not flag it. Viewers who notice feel clever. Viewers who don't get a
  genuine jolt at the payoff. Both rewatch.
- **Geography is a character.** Show the map here, once, properly. The viewer should be able
  to picture where everything is before the story starts moving.

Retention risk: this is where curiosity from the hook decays. Counter it with a **loop line**
around 1:30 — a single sentence that reaches forward: *"Four hours later, none of this would
make sense."*

---

## Beat 4 — The rupture (2:30–5:00)

The discovery. The break. The moment the record stops matching the world.

**Deliver it in the order it was discovered, not the order it is now understood.** This is
the most important craft rule in the entire format. The people who found the scene did not
know what they were looking at. Put the viewer in their position and the strangeness
arrives naturally, without a narrator having to announce that it is strange.

- Lead with sensory and physical facts. What was found, in what condition, where.
- Inventory is your best friend. Lists of ordinary objects in an impossible arrangement are
  more unsettling than any adjective.
- Hold the first "official" reaction until the end of the beat — the moment the
  professionals admit they don't understand is your Act I curtain.
- Insert the first **epistemic marker** here: distinguish the inquiry's findings from the
  press reports. Viewers start trusting you at this exact point.

---

## Beat 5 — Escalation (5:00–8:15)

Three beats. Each one must **close one explanation and open two questions.**

| Sub-beat | Time | Function |
|----------|------|----------|
| 5a | 5:00–6:05 | The obvious explanation, and why it fails |
| 5b | 6:05–7:10 | A new fact that changes the shape of the problem |
| 5c | 7:10–8:15 | The detail that nobody has ever accounted for |

This is where a documentary and a listicle diverge. A listicle stacks strange facts. A
documentary makes each fact *cost* you an explanation you were relying on. The viewer should
feel the ground narrowing.

**Tools for this beat:**
- The search and the failure of the search. Concrete numbers: how many people, how many
  days, how much area.
- One direct quotation, read differently — a slight change in delivery, on screen as a
  document plate.
- The **pivot line** at roughly 40% through the video: *"But there's a problem with that."*
  Use it exactly once. Retention graphs typically show a bump here.
- Present tense for one 30-second reconstruction.

---

## Beat 6 — Revelation (8:15–10:30)

Give the strongest available answer, honestly — **including when the answer is mundane.**

This is a counter-intuitive but load-bearing rule. Channels that refuse to engage with the
boring explanation get destroyed in the comments and never build authority. Channels that
steelman the mundane explanation and *then* show precisely what it fails to cover are
trusted, and the residual mystery is far more powerful for having survived scrutiny.

Order of operations:

1. **State the leading explanation as its best advocate would.** No sneering.
2. **Show what it explains well.** Be generous.
3. **Show the specific thing it does not explain.** One thing, named precisely.
4. **If a genuine resolution exists, give it.** Some of the best episodes end with an answer
   — the DNA result, the declassified file, the confession. A solved mystery told properly
   is more satisfying than an unsolved one told vaguely.

If the story has a real twist — a scientific finding, a document release, an identification
— this is where it lands, at roughly 70–75% through. That placement is deliberate: late
enough to be a payoff, early enough that people stay for the implications.

---

## Beat 7 — Ending (10:30–11:50)

Do not summarise. The viewer just watched it.

The ending does three things in about 210 words:

1. **Name the unanswered question.** One sentence. The specific thing nobody knows.
2. **Return to the image from the hook.** The same street, the same room, the same coastline
   — now meaning something different. This is what makes a video feel authored.
3. **A last line that stops.** Short. No moral. No "we may never know" — that phrase is dead.
   Prefer a fact: *"The file is still open. It has been open for eighty-three years."*

Then a **specific** comment prompt, if any — not "let me know what you think" but
*"If anyone reading this has access to the 1955 inquiry appendices, I'd like to see them."*
Specific prompts get real comments; generic ones get nothing and cost you two seconds of
retention.

**No outro. No subscribe beg.** A four-second end card with the mark, the coordinates, and
one clickable next video. The next-video choice is a decision, not an afterthought: pick the
episode whose *thumbnail* pairs best, not the newest one.

## The retention toolkit

Eight named devices. Every script uses at least five, and the shot list marks where each one
fires.

| Device | What it is | Frequency |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| **Open loop** | Name something you will return to, then move on | Every 90 seconds |
| **The pivot** | "But there's a problem with that." | Once, at ~40% |
| **Withheld detail** | Plant in Setup, pay off in Revelation | Once |
| **Concrete object** | Anchor any abstract stretch to a physical thing on screen | Wherever abstraction appears |
| **Timestamp reset** | A mono card with date/time to re-orient and re-focus attention | 3–4 per video |
| **Direct address** | Break the fourth wall once, at ~60%, to acknowledge the viewer's obvious question | Exactly once |
| **The hard reset** | A total change of visual and sonic register — map sequence, document sequence, or two seconds of silence | Every 2–3 minutes |
| **Present-tense run** | Switch to present tense for a 30-second reconstruction | 2–3 per video |

### Anti-patterns

- No recap of anything said in the last four minutes.
- No "before we get into it."
- No sponsor before the 60% mark. If you take sponsorship, place it immediately after the
  Revelation — the audience has been paid off and will tolerate 40 seconds.
- No music swell to signal that something is important. Let the fact be important.
- No countdown, no ranking, no "number three will surprise you" structure — ever.
- No question in the title that the video does not seriously attempt to answer.

---

## Visual pacing rule

Something on screen changes at least every 8 seconds — a cut, a push-in arriving, a graphic
element drawing on, a document line highlighting. Never leave a static frame under moving
narration for longer than that.

The inverse is also a rule: **when you want a line to land, stop everything.** Static frame,
no music, one second before and after. Once per video, at the ending.

---

## The diagnostic use of the formula

Because every video has the same skeleton, your retention graphs become comparable across
the whole channel. Keep a log (`templates/retention-log.md`) and read it like this:

| Where the drop is | What it means | Fix |
|-------------------|---------------|-----|
| Steep drop before 0:30 | Hook doesn't match the thumbnail promise, or opens with context | Rewrite hook to start inside a moment; re-check title/thumbnail/hook alignment |
| Drop at 0:33–1:00 | Title card is too long, or Setup opens with background | Cut the card to 6s; start Setup on a person, not a place |
| Slow bleed 1:00–2:30 | Setup is delivering information, not stakes | Add human detail; add a loop line at 1:30 |
| Cliff at 5:00 | Escalation restates rather than escalates | Each sub-beat must remove an explanation |
| Drop at 8:15 | Revelation landed as an anticlimax | Move the twist later; steelman the mundane answer harder |
| Sharp drop at 10:30 | Ending reads as an outro | Cut the summary; go straight to the unanswered question |
