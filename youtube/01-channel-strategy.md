# 01 — Channel Strategy

## 1. Ten possible channel names

| # | Name | Register | What it promises |
|---|------|----------|------------------|
| 1 | **Last Known Position** | Search-and-rescue / navigational | The final verified point before a story goes dark |
| 2 | **Grid North** | Cartographic / technical | The world is slightly off from where you think it is |
| 3 | **The Quiet Record** | Archival / restrained | Documents, not screaming |
| 4 | **The Unlit Map** | Exploratory | Places and events outside the light |
| 5 | **Pale Archive** | Institutional / cold | A collection of things nobody filed properly |
| 6 | **Cold Latitude** | Geographic / remote | Isolation, distance, hostile places |
| 7 | **Nightjar** | Naturalist / eerie | A single strange word; ownable, atmospheric |
| 8 | **Unaccounted For** | Bureaucratic / human | Missing people, missing hours, missing answers |
| 9 | **Blackwater Atlas** | Nautical / geographic | Deep, dark, mapped-but-unknown |
| 10 | **The Long Silence** | Elegiac | What happens after the search is called off |

Two of these are deliberately *narrow* (Unaccounted For, The Long Silence) and two are
deliberately *abstract* (Grid North, Nightjar). Narrow names sell the first video harder.
Abstract names survive year three, when you want to make an episode about a cave painting
and a name about missing people no longer fits.

## 2. The strongest three

### Recommended: **Last Known Position**

"Last known position" is the real term search-and-rescue coordinators use for the final
confirmed location of a vessel, aircraft or person before contact was lost. It is a phrase
with a job.

- **It states the format, not the topic.** Every episode is the reconstruction of a record
  up to the point the record stops. That covers disappearances, abandoned places,
  unexplained signals, objects with no origin, and events whose causes were never
  established. It does not lock you into missing persons.
- **It carries built-in dread without a single adjective.** No "dark," no "mysterious," no
  "unexplained." Restraint in the name is a promise about the narration.
- **It gives you a sign-off and a franchise.** Videos can close on a card reading
  `LAST KNOWN POSITION` over coordinates. Series names fall out of it easily:
  *Sea Files*, *Cold Rooms*, *Signal Logs*.
- **Thumbnail behaviour:** stacks cleanly as three short lines in a corner, and the
  initials `LKP` work as a small mark on every frame.
- **Risk:** three words is long for a handle. Secure `@lastknownposition` first; fall back
  to `@LKPfilms` or `@lastknownpos`.

### Runner-up: **Grid North**

Grid north is the direction the vertical lines on a map point. It is not true north and it
is not magnetic north — every map you have ever used is quietly a few degrees wrong, and
navigators correct for it. As a channel name it says: *the accepted version is slightly
off, and this channel measures the difference.*

Choose this one if you want maximum topical freedom and a brand that reads more
*intelligent* than *spooky*. It is the better ten-year name and the weaker cold-start name
— it tells a scrolling stranger nothing about the content, so the thumbnail has to do
100% of the work. Take it only if you are confident in your packaging.

### Third: **The Quiet Record**

The strongest *positioning* name of the ten. The whole competitive opening in this niche
right now is that AI-assisted mystery channels are loud, padded and unsourced. A name that
opens with the word "quiet" is a thesis statement: measured pace, primary documents, no
manufactured shock. It also makes an excellent narration sign-off — "This has been the
quiet record" — and gives you a natural on-screen device (a literal record card with
date, place, source).

Weakness: "quiet" is a hard sell against a hype-trained recommendation feed, and the name
suggests archives more than places or signals.

**Decision:** go with **Last Known Position** unless you have a strong instinct otherwise.
Register the handle on YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, X and a `.com` on the same day.

## 3. Target audience

### The core viewer

A 25–44 year old in the US, UK, Canada or Australia who watches long-form video the way
their parents watched documentaries — on a TV or a phone propped up, in the evening,
alone, usually between 8pm and 1am, often as the last thing before sleep. They are
roughly 65/35 male but the female share is meaningful and rises on human-story episodes
(disappearances, communities, institutional failure) and falls on hardware episodes
(signals, ships, cryptography).

They already watch LEMMiNO, Fascinating Horror, Barely Sociable, Scary Interesting,
Plainly Difficult, Nexpo, Wendigoon, Bedtime Stories, and the better end of the
disaster-analysis niche. They also watch actual documentaries and will compare you to
them, not to other faceless channels.

### What they actually want

1. **To learn something true.** The dopamine here is *"I now know a real thing that most
   people don't."* They will forward the video to one friend with the words "how have we
   never heard of this."
2. **To be treated as an adult.** No repeated explanations. No "as I mentioned earlier."
   No twenty seconds of throat-clearing before the story.
3. **To be told what is not known.** This is the single most under-served desire in the
   niche. This audience has been burned repeatedly by narrators who present speculation in
   the same voice as fact. Saying *"this part is documented; this part is a theory; this
   part nobody knows"* is not a caveat that slows the video down — it is the differentiator
   that makes them subscribe.

### What makes them leave

- Padding. They can smell a story stretched to hit ten minutes.
- A narrator performing emotion the material hasn't earned.
- Obvious AI tells: melting hands, six-fingered crowds, a voice that breathes in the wrong
  places, three sentences in a row starting the same way.
- Being lied to. One fabricated detail that a viewer knows is wrong costs you that viewer
  permanently, and they will say so in the comments where everyone can see.

### Commercial profile

Tier-1 English-speaking, 25–44, long watch sessions, evening viewing. This is one of the
better-monetising demographics on the platform. Documentary/history/mystery content in
this profile typically runs in the mid-to-high single digit RPM range, and higher in Q4.
Long videos with mid-rolls compound that.

The counterweight: this subject matter sits close to YouTube's advertiser-friendly
boundaries around death, crime and tragedy. See `03-scripting-standards.md` for the
handling rules that keep episodes fully monetised without softening the reporting.

### Anti-audience

You are **not** making content for: the 13–17 "scary top 10" audience, the paranormal-
believer audience who want confirmation rather than evidence, or the conspiracy audience.
Serving any of these will change your comment section within a month and you will not get
it back. Politely decline them in the narration by simply being accurate.

## 4. Visual identity

### Palette

Locked, four colours plus black. Every frame, thumbnail and graphic uses only these.

| Role | Hex | Use |
|------|-----|-----|
| Base | `#0A0C0D` | Backgrounds, letterbox bars, card fields |
| Shadow | `#161B1E` | Panel fills, map basemap, secondary surfaces |
| Cold mid | `#3A464C` | Rules, borders, map contours, inactive text |
| Bone | `#E6E1D6` | All primary text, map labels, subtitles |
| Signal | `#C8681E` | Exactly one accent — the amber of a lamp, a warning light, a marker pin |

The discipline that matters: **the accent colour appears once per frame, on the thing you
want looked at.** A pin on a map. A circle round the anomalous detail in a photograph. A
single word in a document. If amber is on two things at once, it points at nothing.

Blacks are lifted slightly (never crushed to pure `#000`) — this reads as film rather than
as a PNG on a black background, and it stops OLED phone screens from swallowing your
shadow detail.

### Typography

| Role | Face | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Display / titles | **Archivo** (Bold, tight tracking) or **Archivo Condensed** | Neo-grotesque, authoritative, free on Google Fonts, condenses well into thumbnails |
| Data / cards / lower-thirds | **JetBrains Mono** | Dates, coordinates, times, case numbers, source lines. Monospace is the whole "record" aesthetic in one choice |
| Quotations / document text | **Newsreader** (Italic for quotes) | Reads as printed page, not as UI |
| Subtitles | **Inter** (Medium, 4% tracking) | Legibility first |

Three faces maximum, no exceptions. The monospace is doing more identity work than the
display face — resist the urge to add a fourth "atmospheric" font.

### The grade

- Desaturate globally to roughly 70–80%, then let the amber accent sit at full strength.
  The eye reads this as "colour has been removed from the world except where it matters."
- Lift the black point slightly; roll off highlights with a soft halation bloom.
- 35mm grain at low opacity, held constant across AI shots, archival footage and graphics
  alike. **This is the single most effective anti-AI-slop tool you have** — a shared grain
  and grade layer over every source type makes generated, archival and graphic material
  feel like one film.
- Very slight gate weave and edge softening on anything presented as archival.
- 16:9 throughout, with a 2.39:1 matte reserved for cold opens and the final beat only, so
  the letterbox means something when it appears.

### Motion language

- Push-ins of 2–4% over 8–10 seconds. Nothing faster.
- Average shot length 4–7 seconds — roughly double the faceless-channel norm. Slower cutting
  reads as confidence and lets the narration lead.
- No whip pans, no zoom punches, no shake, no glitch transitions.
- Cuts land on narration beats and on the *end* of a thought, not mid-clause.
- Silence is a tool. A two-second hold on a still image with no music, once per video, at
  the pivot, is worth more than any sound effect.

### Recurring graphic devices

These five, reused every episode, are what make the channel recognisable in three seconds:

1. **The record card** — full-bleed base colour, mono text: place, date, time, coordinates.
   Opens every act.
2. **The map system** — one template. Dark basemap, bone contour lines, thin cold-mid
   graticule, a single amber marker, animated route drawn on. Never a Google Maps
   screenshot.
3. **The document plate** — a real scan on a base-colour field, with one line circled or
   underlined in amber and a mono source caption bottom-left.
4. **The timeline strip** — a horizontal mono rule with ticks, used to reset the viewer's
   sense of chronology when a story jumps.
5. **The closing frame** — the channel mark, coordinates, and a single line of unanswered
   question, held for four seconds in silence.

### Sound identity

- Three-layer bed: a low sustained drone, a textural layer (room tone, wind, tape hiss),
  and a slow pulse that only enters during escalation.
- Sub-bass hit on each act card. One per act, never more.
- Narration sits around −16 LUFS with the bed ducked 10–12 dB beneath it; master the file
  to roughly −14 LUFS integrated with true peak under −1 dBTP.
- Avoid the licensed trailer-tension tracks that everyone in this niche uses. If you can
  name the track from another channel, do not use it.

### Logo

A wordmark in Archivo Bold, stacked over three lines, with a small surveyor's mark — a
triangle over a dot, or a fine crosshair — set in amber. The mark alone becomes the
avatar; the wordmark is for end cards and the banner.

## 5. Tone of narration

### The persona

Not a storyteller. Not a host. **A researcher who found something in the record and cannot
stop thinking about it.**

That person is calm, precise, occasionally dry, and genuinely uncertain about the parts
that are uncertain. They are interested in the story rather than in your reaction to it.
They never sound like they are trying to keep you watching — which, paradoxically, is why
you keep watching.

### The rules

- **Short sentences.** Average 10–15 words. Vary the rhythm, but let the short ones land
  alone. A four-word sentence after a long one does more than any adjective.
- **150–160 words per minute.** Slower than the platform norm of 175–190. Slow reads as
  authority. It also gives your visuals room to breathe.
- **Concrete over abstract, always.** Not "conditions were harsh" — "the temperature that
  night was −11°C and the nearest road was fourteen miles away."
- **One adjective per idea, maximum.** "Cold, dark, silent, forbidding" is four words doing
  the job of none.
- **Past tense for the record. Present tense for reconstruction.** Switching into present
  tense for a 30-second sequence is your strongest tension device. Use it two or three
  times per video, never more.
- **Names and numbers build trust.** Use the actual name of the officer, the actual hull
  number, the actual time on the log. Specificity is the texture of truth.
- **Never ask a rhetorical question you're about to answer.** "So what happened next?" is
  filler. Just say what happened next.
- **Pauses:** 0.6–0.8s between paragraphs, 1.2–1.5s at act breaks. Write them into the
  script as `[beat]` and `[hold]`.

### The epistemic ladder — the channel's signature

Four registers, used with deliberately consistent phrasing so viewers learn to hear the
difference:

| Register | Phrasing | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Documented** | "The record shows…" / "According to the inquiry…" / "This is confirmed." | "The inquiry recorded that the radio was tuned to the international distress frequency." |
| **Reported, unverified** | "It was reported at the time that…" / "This detail appears in the newspaper accounts but not in the official file." | "Later accounts describe a briefcase. The inventory does not mention one." |
| **Theory** | "The most common explanation is…" / "This is a theory, and it explains X, but not Y." | "The most common explanation is a rogue wave. That accounts for the list. It does not account for the missing cargo." |
| **Unknown** | "Nobody knows." / "There is no record of…" / "This has never been explained." | "Nobody knows why he stopped." |

Say "nobody knows" out loud, at least once, in every video. It is the phrase that separates
this channel from the rest of the niche.

### The forbidden list

Never write, and never let a draft keep:

> "Welcome back to the channel" · "In this video we'll be exploring" · "But first" · "Little
> did they know" · "What happened next will" · "You won't believe" · "Buckle up" ·
> "Let that sink in" · "The truth is stranger than fiction" · "Chilling" · "Bone-chilling"
> · "Spine-tingling" · "Eerie" (more than once) · "Bizarre" (more than once) · "Mysterious"
> (more than twice) · "Little is known about" · "To this day" · "Some say" ·
> "It is said that" · "Many believe" · "One thing is certain" · "The plot thickens" ·
> "Delve" · "Testament to" · "In the annals of" · "Shrouded in mystery"

Also banned: opening two consecutive sentences with the same word; the three-item rhetorical
list ("no motive, no witnesses, no answers") more than once per video; and any sentence
that could be swapped into a different episode without changing a word.

### Voice casting

A warm, low, unhurried voice. Received-Pronunciation-adjacent British reads as
"documentary" to a global audience and is the safest default; a neutral North American
read also works and slightly widens US appeal. The essential quality is **under-performance**
— someone reading a report they find interesting, not someone narrating a horror trailer.

If you use AI narration (see `05-production-workflow.md` for settings), the two things
that give it away are over-uniform pacing and wrong-place breaths. Both are fixable in the
edit, and both must be fixed. When revenue allows, a real human voice is the first upgrade
you should buy.

## 6. Recommended video length

**Target: 11–14 minutes. Script length 1,700–2,200 words.**

The reasoning:

- **Over 8 minutes** unlocks multiple mid-roll ad breaks. Below that you are leaving the
  majority of your revenue on the table for no retention benefit.
- **11–14 minutes** is long enough to build a real three-act reconstruction and short
  enough that a new channel can hold 45–55% average viewing. A 25-minute video from a
  channel with no audience trust tends to bleed viewers in the middle third, and the
  resulting low average-view-duration is a worse signal than a shorter video watched well.
- **Watch-time maths:** a 12-minute video at 50% AVD delivers 6 minutes per view. That is
  the number that gets you to 4,000 hours.

Hard rule: **never pad to hit a runtime.** If the story is genuinely a nine-minute story,
publish nine minutes. A tight nine outperforms a bloated thirteen every time, and the
audience above can tell the difference instantly.

**The quarterly flagship:** once every 10–13 videos, make one 25–35 minute deep
reconstruction of your single best story. These do three things a weekly episode can't:
they get compared to real documentaries, they generate enormous watch-time per view, and
they are the videos people link to when recommending the channel. Budget three times the
normal effort. Reserve one of your strongest topics for it rather than burning it early.

## 7. Recommended upload schedule

**One video per week, Friday, 16:00 UK / 11:00 US Eastern. Same slot every week.**

- **Weekly is the correct cadence.** Twice-weekly is not sustainable at this quality level
  and the second video cannibalises the first. Fortnightly is too slow for the algorithm to
  learn who your audience is during the critical first three months.
- **Friday afternoon** publishes into the strongest window for this genre: US East Coast
  lunchtime and after-work, UK evening, and the whole of the weekend for the recommendation
  system to work with. Mystery and documentary viewing peaks Friday through Sunday night.
- **The slot must be identical every week.** Returning viewers are a bigger factor in this
  niche than in most, and they need a habit to form around.

### Buffer discipline

- **Do not publish until four finished videos exist.** This is non-negotiable. The buffer
  is what protects the schedule the first time you get ill, get stuck on a fact-check, or
  a story collapses under research.
- Maintain the buffer at three-to-four permanently. Every week you publish one and finish
  one.
- If the buffer ever drops to one, publish a shorter, simpler story that week rather than
  breaking the schedule.

### Weekly rhythm

| Day | Work | Hours |
|-----|------|-------|
| Mon | Research dossier + beat sheet for video N+4 | 2.5 |
| Tue | Draft script, human voice pass, fact-check pass | 2.5 |
| Wed | Shot list, batch asset generation (mostly unattended), voiceover record | 2.0 |
| Thu | Edit assembly, sound, grade, captions | 3.0 |
| Fri | Package (thumbnail, title, description), publish video N, first 4 hours of comments | 1.5 |

Roughly 11.5 hours in week one, settling to 6–8 once your template library is built.

### Shorts

Do not start Shorts until eight long-form videos are live. When you do:

- Maximum two per week, and they must look like the channel — same grade, same fonts, same
  voice, same restraint.
- They are **standalone strange facts**, not clips. A 45-second Short that ends on an
  unanswered question converts; a 45-second clip ripped from the middle of a video does not.
- No "watch the full video" card. Let the channel page do the work.
- Track subscriber conversion separately. If Shorts subscribers are not watching long-form
  after six weeks, stop making them — a subscriber who only watches Shorts actively damages
  your long-form impressions.
